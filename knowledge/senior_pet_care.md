# Senior Pet Care Considerations

Dogs are generally considered senior starting around age 7, though large and
giant breeds age faster and may be considered senior as early as 5–6. Cats are
typically considered senior starting around age 11. Senior pets benefit from
more frequent veterinary checkups — often every 6 months instead of annually —
since age-related conditions can progress quickly if undetected.

Mobility changes are common in senior pets: reduced flexibility, stiffness
after rest, and reluctance to jump or climb stairs can indicate arthritis.
Shorter, more frequent activity sessions are usually better tolerated than one
long walk or play session, and non-slip flooring or ramps can help reduce
strain on joints.

Cognitive decline can affect senior pets similarly to dementia in humans,
presenting as confusion, disrupted sleep cycles, house-soiling in previously
trained pets, or increased vocalization at night. These behavioral changes are
worth noting and discussing with a vet rather than assuming they're simple
"bad behavior."

Senior pets often need dietary adjustments — lower calorie density to prevent
weight gain from reduced activity, but higher-quality protein to preserve
muscle mass, along with joint-support supplements like glucosamine in some
cases. Dental disease also becomes more common with age and can cause pain
that reduces appetite, so declining interest in food shouldn't be assumed to
be behavioral without ruling out dental issues first.
